# Career Tracks — Eight Written Courses for Tech Interviews in Egypt

**Goal:** walk into an entry- to mid-level interview for any of eight roles, which are **frontend, backend, full-stack, data analyst, data scientist, data engineer, AI engineer and network & connectivity engineer**, knowing the fundamentals correctly, using the vocabulary precisely, and able to tell your own project stories well.

**One collection, three origins.** The six software and data tracks were written for this handbook. The **AI Engineer** track is *The AI Journey Handbook* (modules **AI0**–**AI25**) and the **Network & Connectivity Engineer** track is the *Connectivity Interview Bootcamp* (modules **N0**–**N16**). Both keep their own text, numbering and depth; here they gain the shared sidebar, search, level filter, glossary, progress tracking and a self-test.

Pick a track above. The sidebar then shows that track's path in order, from the basics to the interview hub at the end. You can switch tracks at any time; your progress is saved per module in this browser, so a module you finish on one track counts on every track that shares it.

## How to use this handbook 🟢

| Step | Do this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pick your **track** and your **target level** (the level buttons at the top). | The level filter hides sections you can skip for now. |
| 2 | Open each module and read its **Interview focus** card first. | It says what each level must be able to do and lists the most-asked questions. |
| 3 | Read the **Say it in the interview** boxes aloud. | Each is a ready one-to-two-sentence answer. Speaking the answer is the skill being tested. |
| 4 | Do the **Try it** boxes. | Twenty minutes of doing turns "I understand" into "I have done it", which interviewers trust far more. |
| 5 | Finish each module with its **Interview drill** flash cards and **Key takeaways**. | Answer the card out loud before you reveal it. |
| 6 | When the path is done, take the track's **Self-test** (sidebar, under Reference). | Every wrong answer links back to the section to reread. |

**System design is on every track.** Every path has a *System design* stage built from five shared modules: [[SD1]] fundamentals (the building blocks, the numbers, availability), [[SD2]] scaling in production from one server to millions, [[SD3]] how to choose technologies and defend the choice, [[SD4]] reliability and distributed-system patterns, and [[SD5]] system design applied to each role (frontend, analyst, data engineer, data scientist, AI engineer, network engineer). Every track gets the fundamentals; backend, full-stack, data engineering and AI go deepest.

**Overlaps, so you never study the same thing twice.** The shared modules (code **S1**–**S10** and **SD1**–**SD5**) appear on several paths. Full-stack reuses most of the frontend and backend modules and adds five of its own that join the two halves. The three data tracks share SQL, Python and statistics. The AI and network tracks are complete courses of their own; where they cover the same ground as a shared module (SQL in [[AI12]], statistics in [[AI15]], Linux in [[N11]]), either one will do.

## How hiring usually works in Egypt 🟢 ⭐

The detail is in [[S8]]; this is the shape.

1. **Application and screening.** Most postings arrive through LinkedIn, Wuzzuf and company career pages, and through referrals, which carry far more weight than cold applications. An HR call checks your English, notice period, salary expectation and, for men, **military status**. Have "Exempted" ready, with the certificate date.
2. **Technical screen.** Usually 30–60 minutes with an engineer: definitions, "explain your project", and a few deeper "why" questions. The **Interview focus** card in each module targets this round.
3. **Coding or task.** A live problem (shared editor or a platform such as HackerRank or Codility), a **take-home task** (common in Egyptian startups, typically a small CRUD app or a data analysis with a few days to submit), or both.
4. **Team or manager round.** Architecture of your past work, behavioural questions, "why us".
5. **Offer.** Gross versus net salary, the social insurance deduction, the probation period (commonly three months), and benefits such as medical insurance. These are covered in [[S8]].

> [!warning] On salary figures
> This handbook deliberately quotes no salary numbers. Published figures for Egypt vary widely between sources, change quickly with inflation and the exchange rate, and are mostly self-reported. Before a negotiation, check several current postings that show a range, and ask people one or two years ahead of you in the same kind of company.

## Study plans by time available 🟢

| Your situation | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Interview in 3 days** | The track's last module (its interview hub) first, then [[S8]], then only the 🟢 sections of the modules the job description names. Take the self-test on the last evening. |
| **Two weeks** | Follow your track's path in order with the level filter on **Entry only**. One module a day, two on light days. Do every *Try it*. |
| **Four to six weeks** | The full path at **Entry + Mid**. Close one portfolio gap from the track's interview hub in parallel, so you have something new to talk about. |
| **After you are hired** | The 🔴 sections, and the "State of the art" boxes, become your 90-day reading list. |

**Rule for every plan:** a 🔴 section is "know the idea in one sentence" unless the job is that specialism.

## Five ideas that carry every track 🟢 ⭐

| # | Idea | Say it like this |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Start from the user's or the business's problem.** | "Before choosing a tool I asked what decision this would change and how we'd know it worked." |
| 2 | **Measure, don't guess.** | "I benchmarked ADO.NET, Dapper and EF Core before choosing; EF Core cost about 6% more time and nearly double the memory, which was acceptable for our CRUD screens." |
| 3 | **Make it correct, then make it fast, then make it pretty.** | "I wrote the tests that pin the behaviour first, so the optimisation couldn't silently change results." |
| 4 | **Every boundary needs a contract.** | "The API, the database schema and the CSV import each had an explicit contract, and validation lived at the boundary." |
| 5 | **Explain trade-offs, not just choices.** | "I picked a modular monolith over microservices because a team of six didn't need independent deploys yet." |

## Who this is written for

You: a computer systems engineer (Greenwich and MSA, 2024) who finished the ITI intensive .NET track, led the backend and AI work on FinSight, works as a tech specialist intern at Beeviro, and supported Oracle Forms screens and users at Farwaniya Hospital. The handbook leans on that:

- **Analogies from your own stack.** Middleware is a pipeline you have built; a dimensional model is the `DailyAggregatedTransaction` table grown up; a React effect is Angular's `ngOnInit` plus `ngOnDestroy` in one place.
- **Your projects as answers.** The **From your own work** boxes turn each concept into something you can say "I did".
- **Honest gaps.** Where your evidence is thin, the module says so and gives the cheapest way to close it, in line with `ATS gaps to fill.md` in your CV folder.

## What this is not

It is not a replacement for building things. Every module has at least one *Try it*. The handbook gets you to "I can explain it correctly"; the labs get you to "I've done it", and a deployed project gets you to "here's the link".

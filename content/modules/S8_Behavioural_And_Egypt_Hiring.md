# Behavioural Interviews, Your Story, and Getting Hired in Egypt

Technical rounds decide whether you *can* do the job; behavioural rounds decide whether they *want* you to. In Egyptian companies the HR call and the manager round weigh heavily, and many candidates lose offers they'd technically earned because they told their story badly. This module gives you a ready story for each track, a bank of eight stories from your real work, model answers to the questions that always come, and the practical details of Egyptian offers.

> [!focus]
> **Entry must:** a 60-second "tell me about yourself" tailored to the role; four or five STAR stories; a clear walkthrough of one project; good questions to ask; a salary expectation you can say calmly.
> **Mid adds:** stories about ownership, disagreement, mentoring and trade-offs; negotiating an offer; reading a contract.
> **Most asked:** *Tell me about yourself* · *Walk me through your project* · *Hardest bug?* · *A conflict with a teammate?* · *A failure?* · *Why us?* · *Your salary expectation?* · *Your military status?* · *Do you have questions for us?*
> **Time budget:** 3 hours to prepare, then rehearse out loud until it's natural.

## S8.0 Foundations: what interviewers are actually scoring 🟢

Most companies with a real hiring process use a **structured interview**: each interviewer has a short list of competencies, asks questions designed to reveal them, and fills in a **scorecard** afterwards, usually with a rating per competency and evidence quoted from your answers. Two things follow:

- **Evidence beats adjectives.** "I'm a team player" scores nothing; "I paired with the author of the conflicting branch, we agreed a folder convention, and the conflicts stopped" is evidence for collaboration *and* ownership.
- **Every question has a target.** Know what each common question is testing and make sure your answer delivers it.

Interviewers also listen for **signals that cut across every answer**: you say "I" for your own work and "we" for the team's; you name trade-offs instead of claiming everything went perfectly; you're specific (numbers, names of tools, what changed); and you stop when you've answered.

## S8.1 How hiring works here 🟢 ⭐

**Where the jobs come from:**

- **LinkedIn** and **Wuzzuf** are the main boards for tech roles in Egypt; Bayt and company career pages add more. Set job alerts with the exact titles from your CVs.
- **Referrals** beat cold applications by a wide margin. Your ITI cohort, your FinSight teammates, your Beeviro colleagues and your Greenwich/MSA classmates are a network: tell them which roles you want.
- **Recruiters and agencies** for outsourcing and multinational centres.
- **Remote work for foreign companies**, directly or through platforms. The interviews are the same shape, with more weight on written English and working asynchronously.

**Kinds of employer, and what their interviews stress:**

| Employer type | Examples (illustrative) | Interviews tend to stress |
|---|---|---|
| Product startups and fintechs | Payments, delivery, lending and brokerage apps | Take-home tasks, ownership, speed, system design at mid level |
| Multinational engineering centres | Automotive, telecom and enterprise-software centres in Cairo and Giza | Fundamentals, OOP, structured process, English |
| Software houses and outsourcing | Firms building for Gulf and European clients | Stack fit (.NET, Angular, React), CRUD fluency, client communication |
| Banks, telecoms and government projects | Large IT departments | SQL, reliability, process, security; Oracle and .NET are common |
| Data and analytics teams | Telecoms, banks, e-commerce, consultancies | SQL above all, Power BI or Python, business sense |

**The loop:** application → HR screen (English, availability, notice period, salary expectation, military status) → technical screen → coding round or take-home task → team or manager round → offer. Expect two to four weeks end to end; follow up politely after a week of silence.

> [!note] Military status
> Egyptian employers ask male candidates for it at screening, and many postings filter on it. You are **permanently exempted** (March 2025). Say it in one breath, "Exempted, I have the certificate", and keep the certificate scan ready to send.

## S8.2 "Tell me about yourself" — one per track 🟢 ⭐

The formula, in about 60 seconds: **present** (who you are now) → **past** (two pieces of proof that matter for *this* role) → **future** (why this job is the next step). No life history, no list of every technology.

> [!say] Backend / full-stack
> "I'm a .NET developer. I finished ITI's intensive .NET track this year and I'm currently a tech specialist intern at Beeviro. My main project was FinSight, an AI virtual CFO built by six of us, where I was the lead contributor: I owned the ASP.NET Core backend, with multi-tenancy through EF Core query filters, JWT auth, background forecasting with Hangfire and real-time updates with SignalR, and I deployed it in Docker on Azure. Before that I supported a hospital information system in Kuwait, so I'm used to real users depending on the system. I'm looking for a backend role where I can own features end to end in a team that cares about quality."

> [!say] Frontend
> "I'm a frontend-leaning full-stack developer. I've built Angular apps, FinSight's dashboard with signals and Chart.js, and a fractional-investment platform on Angular 21, and React apps with TypeScript, like CS Visualizer, which animates code execution step by step. I care about the parts users feel: accessibility, a responsive layout, and keeping the UI fast and predictable. I also built a bilingual English and Arabic site, so I've handled right-to-left layouts properly. I'm looking for a frontend role where design and engineering work closely."

> [!say] Data analyst
> "I'm an engineer who likes turning data into decisions. I studied data science and machine learning after my degree, and my capstone analysed UK road-accident data: cleaning, statistical tests and explaining what drives severe accidents. On FinSight I wrote Python scripts that pulled progress reports out of Jira for the team, and I built the data layer that aggregates transactions daily. I'm strong in SQL and Python, and I'm building my Power BI portfolio now. I want an analyst role where I'm close to the business questions."

> [!say] Data scientist
> "I build and evaluate predictive models end to end. My graduation project, Emotidect, recognised emotion in speech using a HuBERT transformer, and we validated it on Egyptian-Arabic speech before British and Egyptian juries. In my data-science course capstone I handled heavy class imbalance, compared models fairly and used SHAP to explain them; and on FinSight I integrated TimeGPT forecasts into a production API. I'm looking for a data-science role where models actually reach users."

> [!say] Data engineer
> "I'm a software engineer moving into data engineering. On FinSight I built the ingestion side: CSV imports with a transaction categoriser, a daily aggregation layer that fills calendar gaps so forecasting has a continuous series, and nightly Hangfire jobs, all on SQL Server in Docker. I studied the Hadoop and Spark ecosystem through Huawei's big-data material, and I'm strongest at the software side of data: reliable, tested, deployable code. I want a role building pipelines that other teams can trust."

## S8.3 The STAR method and your story bank 🟢 ⭐

> [!term] STAR
> **S**ituation (one or two sentences of context) → **T**ask (what *you* had to do) → **A**ction (what you did, in the first person, with detail: most of the answer) → **R**esult (what changed, and what you learned). Aim for 90 seconds to two minutes.

Prepare these eight stories once; each answers several questions.

| # | Story (from your real work) | Answers questions about |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Merging every FinSight branch.** Six people, constant conflicts; you took on integration, talked to authors before resolving, and set conventions | Teamwork, conflict, leadership, Git |
| 2 | **The sparse-data forecasting problem.** TimeGPT rejected tenants with gaps; you designed the daily aggregation layer with gap-filling and a time shift so forecasts could be checked against later real data | Hardest technical problem, problem solving |
| 3 | **The July hardening sweep.** Before the demo you scrubbed secrets, enforced multi-tenancy with global query filters and hardened auth, prioritising by risk | Ownership, security, working under a deadline |
| 4 | **Thirteen bugs caught by differential tests** in CS Visualizer, by comparing your interpreter's output with real .NET execution | Quality, testing, attention to detail |
| 5 | **Farwaniya Hospital.** Departments called with urgent access problems; you triaged, granted role-based access correctly, turned requirement booklets into Jira tickets, and trained non-technical staff so calls dropped | Customer focus, pressure, communication with non-technical people |
| 6 | **Defending Emotidect** before British and Egyptian juries, and validating on Egyptian-Arabic speech when the training data was Persian | Presenting, handling hard questions, rigour |
| 7 | **Six years teaching at Resala.** Explaining things to people with very different backgrounds | Communication, mentoring, motivation |
| 8 | **A failure:** pick a real one, such as an estimate you missed, a feature that had to be redone, or the fine-tune that wasn't finished, and say what you changed afterwards | Failure, learning, self-awareness |

**Example, story 2, told in STAR:**

> [!say]
> "**Situation:** in FinSight, our cash-flow forecasts came from TimeGPT, but real companies don't have transactions every day, so the API rejected tenants with gaps in their series. **Task:** I owned the AI integration, so I had to make forecasting work for real, sparse data. **Action:** I added a daily aggregation layer that summed transactions per day and inserted zero rows for missing days, so every tenant had a continuous series. I also built in a time shift, so we could forecast from an earlier date and compare against real data that came later, which gave us a way to check accuracy instead of trusting it. **Result:** forecasting worked for every tenant, and the back-test gave us an honest accuracy number for the demo. What I took from it is to validate the shape of data at the boundary before blaming the model."

> [!mistake] "We did…"
> Interviewers are hiring *you*. Say "we" for the team's goal and "I" for your actions. If you can't say what *you* did, the story isn't ready.

## S8.4 The questions that always come 🟢 ⭐

| Question | What they're really asking | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **What's your biggest weakness?** | Self-awareness and growth | A real, non-fatal weakness plus what you're doing about it. "I used to over-polish before asking for feedback; on FinSight I started opening draft pull requests early, and reviews got faster." |
| **Tell me about a conflict.** | Can you disagree without damage? | Story 1. Focus on understanding the other view, the data you used, and the outcome. Never blame. |
| **Tell me about a failure.** | Do you own mistakes? | Story 8. Spend most of the time on what you changed afterwards. |
| **Why do you want to work here?** | Did you research us? | Two specifics about their product, users or engineering (from their site, blog or LinkedIn) and how your experience fits one of them. |
| **Why are you leaving / why now?** | Any red flags? | Positive and forward-looking: "my internship ends", "I want to go deeper into backend work than my current role allows." |
| **Where do you see yourself in five years?** | Will you stay and grow? | Growth in *this* direction: "a strong mid-level backend engineer who owns services and mentors juniors." |
| **Why the gap / the short roles?** | Reliability | Honest and brief: training (ITI, the data-science course) and short contracts (Bishara) were deliberate steps. Then move on. |
| **What's your salary expectation?** | Are we in range? | See [[S8.8]]. Give a researched range, as a net monthly figure, and stay flexible. |
| **Do you have any questions?** | Are you curious and serious? | Always yes. See [[S8.7]]. |

## S8.5 Walking through a project 🟢 ⭐

This question decides most junior interviews. Use the same structure every time:

1. **The problem and the user** (one sentence). "Small companies don't have a CFO; FinSight gives them forecasts and alerts from their own transactions."
2. **Your role and the team.** "Six of us; I owned the backend and the AI layer and integrated everyone's branches."
3. **The architecture**, drawn if possible: Angular → API → SQL Server, LangFlow flows, Hangfire jobs, SignalR, Docker on Azure.
4. **One hard problem**, told in STAR (story 2 or 3).
5. **Trade-offs:** "I chose a modular monolith over microservices because…"; "the LLM never touches the database directly; it goes through our authenticated API, so tenant isolation holds."
6. **What you'd do differently:** "more automated tests earlier; Redis for the dashboard summary; deploy a public demo." That shows judgement, not weakness.

**Then expect the drill-down.** For each technology you name, be ready for "why that, not X?" and "how does it work underneath?". If you say "multi-tenancy with global query filters", be ready to explain how EF Core applies the filter, and how you'd stop an admin query from accidentally ignoring it ([[B5]]).

> [!warning] Only claim what's yours
> Teammates built the Unit of Work layer and Google sign-in on FinSight. If asked, say so: "a teammate built that; I integrated and reviewed it." Interviewers dig, and honesty about boundaries makes everything else you say more credible.

## S8.6 Take-home tasks: how to stand out 🟢 ⭐

Take-homes are common in Egyptian startups: a small API, a small UI, or a data analysis in a few days.

- **Read the brief twice** and list the requirements. Ask one clarifying question by email if something is genuinely ambiguous. That itself scores points.
- **Do the core well before extras.** A complete, tested, simple solution beats an ambitious half-finished one.
- **The README is part of the answer:** how to run it (one command, ideally `docker compose up`), what you built, the decisions and trade-offs, what you'd do with more time, and assumptions you made.
- **Commit as you go** with clear messages, so the history tells the story.
- **Tests** for the core logic, even a few.
- **Time-box** honestly; if they say "about 4 hours", don't hand in 40 hours of polish and imply it took 4.
- For data take-homes: start with the question, show the cleaning decisions, give **three findings and a recommendation** up front, and put methodology after.

## S8.7 Questions to ask them 🟢

Ask two or three; pick ones whose answers you actually want.

- "What does a successful first three months look like in this role?"
- "How does code get from a laptop to production here? How long does it usually take?"
- "How are code reviews done, and who reviews a junior's work?"
- "What's the biggest technical challenge the team is facing this year?"
- For data roles: "Who uses the analyses or models, and how do you know they made a difference?"
- To end: "Is there anything in my background that makes you hesitate? I'd like the chance to address it."

## S8.8 Offers in Egypt: what the numbers mean 🟢 🟡 ⭐

**Gross vs net.** Egyptian offers may be quoted gross (before deductions) or net (take-home). Always confirm which, and compare offers **net per month**.

**Deductions from a salaried employee's pay:**

- **Social insurance** under Law No. 148 of 2019: the employee pays **11%** of the *insurable wage* (the employer pays a further 18.75%). The insurable wage has a floor and a cap that rise every January; for **2026** they are **EGP 2,700 and EGP 16,700 per month**, so above the cap the deduction stops growing.
- **Income tax:** progressive, with a personal exemption. The brackets change with new laws, so use a current Egyptian net-salary calculator, or the Egyptian Tax Authority's own, rather than any number quoted from memory.

**The contract:**

- **Probation** under the new **Labour Law No. 14 of 2025** (in force since **1 September 2025**) may not exceed **three months**, and an employer may apply probation only **once**. A second or extended probation clause is void.
- Check the notice period, working hours and days (Sunday–Thursday is usual), remote or hybrid terms, medical insurance (individual or family), annual raises, bonuses, and equipment.
- **Contractor vs employee:** remote contracts with foreign companies are often freelance or contractor agreements. That usually means no social insurance or medical cover unless you arrange it, and that you handle your own tax. Weigh the higher gross against what you give up.

**Negotiating:**

1. Research a range from several current postings that show salaries and from people one or two years ahead of you. (This handbook deliberately quotes no market figures; they change too fast. See [[00]].)
2. Let them make the first offer when you can; if pushed, give a range whose bottom you'd happily accept.
3. Negotiate the whole package: net salary, start date, review after probation, training budget, certification fees, remote days, equipment.
4. Be warm and specific: "I'm excited about the role. Based on the scope and my experience with production .NET and Azure, I was hoping for closer to X. Is there flexibility?"
5. Get the final offer **in writing** before resigning from anything else.

> [!say]
> "Based on my research for junior .NET roles with production experience, I'm looking for a net monthly salary in the range of X to Y, but the role and the team matter most to me, so I'm open to discussing the full package."

## S8.9 Communication in the interview 🟢

- **English:** clear beats fancy. Short sentences, technical terms used precisely. If you didn't understand a question, say "Could you rephrase that?"; that's normal, even for native speakers.
- **Think aloud** in technical rounds; silence reads as being stuck.
- **"I don't know"** is fine once, if you follow it with how you'd find out or reason toward it: "I haven't used Kafka in production, but I understand it as a partitioned log, so I'd expect…".
- **Online setup:** wired or reliable internet, a quiet room, the camera at eye level, the editor and your project's architecture diagram open in tabs, water nearby. Test the meeting link ten minutes early.
- **Body language:** look at the camera when you speak, smile at the start, and don't read from notes.

## S8.10 After the interview 🟢

- Send a short thank-you message the same day: one line on something specific you discussed.
- Write down every question you were asked, especially the ones you fumbled, and add them to your study list. After five interviews you'll have your own question bank.
- Rejection is normal: application-to-interview ratios of one in seven or worse are common, even for good candidates. Ask for feedback politely; some companies give it. Keep applying in parallel, and never stop while waiting on a single offer.

> [!lab] Rehearse with a recorder
> Record yourself answering "tell me about yourself", "walk me through FinSight" and "tell me about a conflict" on your phone. Listen once. Cut every "basically" and every sentence that doesn't help. Record again. Three rounds of this beats ten silent read-throughs.

## S8.11 Interview drill 🟢 ⭐

| Question | Strong short answer |
|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself. | Present role → two proofs relevant to this job (FinSight ownership, Farwaniya or Emotidect) → why this role is the next step. About 60 seconds. |
| What's STAR? | Situation, Task, Action, Result: structure behavioural answers with most of the time on your own actions. |
| Biggest weakness? | A real but manageable one, plus the concrete habit you've built to handle it. |
| Why should we hire you? | Match three of their requirements to three pieces of evidence, then add one thing that sets you apart (production deployment, users in a hospital, a jury-defended project). |
| Your salary expectation? | A researched net monthly range, said calmly, with openness to the whole package. |
| What's your military status? | Exempted, with the certificate available. |
| What would you do differently on your project? | Name two concrete improvements (earlier tests, caching, a public demo) that show judgement. |
| How long can probation be in Egypt? | At most three months, and only once with the same employer, under Labour Law No. 14 of 2025. |
| What comes off a gross salary? | Employee social insurance at 11% of the insurable wage (capped) and progressive income tax; compare offers net. |
| What questions do you ask at the end? | What success looks like in three months, how code reaches production, how juniors are reviewed, and the team's biggest challenge. |

## Key takeaways

> [!check]
> - One tailored 60-second story per role: present, two proofs, future.
> - Eight real stories in STAR cover nearly every behavioural question.
> - Walk through projects as problem → role → architecture → hard problem → trade-offs → what you'd change.
> - Claim only your own work, and say so clearly.
> - Compare offers net; know the 11% insurance, the 2026 cap, and the three-month probation rule.
